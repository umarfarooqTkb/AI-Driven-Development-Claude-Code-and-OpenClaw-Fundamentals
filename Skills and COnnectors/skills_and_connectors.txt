What is a Skill
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
- Skills are the specific capabilities or tasks that an AI system can perform.
- A skill tells the AI what it knows how to do.
- A Skill is a specialized ability of an AI assistant to perform a specific task, process, or workflow.
- Skill is the AI’s ability to perform the task.
- You have given your skills and working style to AI and AI will do it for you.
- Now AI has to connect with other tools to perform actions, like to connect with Google Drive. Connectors are used for this purpose.
- Skill will be given to AI once and AI will repeat it again and again.
- A chat tells AI what to do once; a Skill teaches it how every time; a Connector gives it hands to reach your apps. (The kitchen: connectors are the kitchen, skills are the recipes.)

- A Skill is a folder with a SKILL.md text file: a name, a description, and plain-English instructions. No code required to start.
- Skill = A folder with a txt file (SKILL.md)
- SKILL.md
   * Name
   * Description
   * Some Rules/Instructions (written in plain English)
- Instructions are important, AI will work as given in instructions
- Calude introduced SKILL and Instructions
- Open Claude >> customized = skills and connectors >> skill creator

- Connector = brings the data & Skill = performs the work
- A Connector is safe, permission-scoped access to an app (Drive, Gmail, Slack, a tracker), running on the MCP standard. AI inherits your permissions.

- Skills are the AI’s abilities to perform tasks.
- Skill: a saved set of instructions that teaches AI how to do one task your way.
- Connectors are integrations that allow AI to access external systems and data.
- Connector: a safe link that lets AI reach one of your apps (files, email, messages).

Together, they make AI useful in real development work:
- Skills help AI think and execute. Connectors help AI access the right information and tools.
- Skill = expertise. Connector = access.

- A chat message tells AI what to do this once.
- A Skill teaches AI how to do something every time. 
- A Connector gives AI hands to reach your real apps and data.

- A Skill without a Connector can explain or format work but may not access your live systems.
- A Connector without a Skill gives Claude access, but Claude may perform the task without your organization’s specific standards.
- Skill + Connector creates a repeatable, operational workflow.

- In Claude.ai, skills fire automatically when your prompt matches their description. 
- Slash commands and naming the skill are override tools, used more in Cowork and the developer apps. So the description is the most important thing you write.

Connectors
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
- Connectors allow the AI system to connect with external tools, systems, data sources, or applications.
- A connector tells the AI where it can get information from or send actions to.
- A Connector is an integration that allows an AI assistant to access or interact with external systems, tools, files, databases, APIs, or platforms.
- Connector: a safe link that lets AI reach one of your apps (files, email, messages).
- Connector: a safe link that lets AI reach one of your apps (files, email, messages).


- A Connector is how AI safely reaches your apps and data.
- Connect Claude to Google Drive and it can search your files; connect it to Gmail and it can read your mail and draft replies for you to send;
   connect it to Slack, Linear, Asana, Notion, and it can pull from and act in those tools.

- Claude Connectors commonly use MCP — Model Context Protocol. 
- MCP is an open standard through which AI applications can connect to external data sources, tools and workflows. 
- The MCP documentation compares it to a USB-C port: one standardized interface that can connect many different systems.

Skills and Connectors in Deep dive
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================

# A skill is a folder with a text file in it.
- The required text file is named SKILL.md (exactly that, capital letters and all).
- It holds two things at the top, a name and a description, and below that, the instructions you want AI to follow.
- That's the minimum.
- A skill can be nothing but a name, a description, and a paragraph of plain-English instructions, and it will work.

- Optionally, the folder can hold more: example files, a template document, reference notes, even small scripts.
- But none of that is required to start.
- The accountant's first skill could be five sentences of formatting rules.
- The marketer's could be their brand voice guide, pasted in. 
- And those optional scripts? They are real code, but you never write a line of it; AI writes any code a skill needs when you build it

- Anthropic's own description: skills are "folders of instructions, scripts, and resources that Claude loads dynamically to improve performance on specialized tasks." 
- The key word is dynamically: 
- AI doesn't carry every skill in its head at all times. 
- It keeps a short list of what each skill is for (that's the description), and only opens the full instructions when your request actually matches one. 
- Engineers call this progressive disclosure, and it is why you can have dozens of skills installed without slowing anything down or confusing the AI. 
- We'll come back to it when you build one.

## The mental model
- A Skill is a recipe card you write once and pin to the kitchen wall.
- Every time that dish is ordered, the cook follows the card: same result, no reminding. 
- You are not in the kitchen, but your standards are.


- Under the hood, connectors run on an open standard called MCP (Model Context Protocol).
-  A connector is a small piece of software (an "MCP server") that a company, or increasingly an AI, built with code. 
- You never build it or read it; you click Connect.
- That's the same bargain the rest of this book calls code you never write: the code is real and working for you, but writing it was never your job.
-  You don't need to understand MCP to use a connector, any more than you need to understand how email is delivered to send one. What matters are three facts:
  1. AI inherits your permissions.
            A connector can never reach something you yourself can't reach. 
            If you don't have access to a file, channel, or record in the original app, the connector can't get to it either. 
            Connecting Google Drive does not hand AI your whole company's drive, only what your own account can already open.
  2. You choose read-only or read-and-write. 
            "Search and summarize my email" is read-only and low-risk. 
            "Send this email" or "create this issue" is a write action. Good practice is to start read-only and grant write access only once you trust how the tool behaves (more in the safety section).
  3. You turn them on per conversation. 
             Connecting an app once makes it available; you still decide which connectors a given chat may use.

  There is a directory of ready-made connectors (Google Drive, Gmail, Slack, Notion, Figma, Linear, Atlassian, and many more), and you can also add a custom connector to any service that speaks MCP. 
  Some connectors are interactive: they render a live dashboard, task board, or design surface right inside the conversation rather than just returning text.




Example Skill (Markdown)
======================================================================================================================================================
---
name: cloud-change-review
description: Review Terraform, Kubernetes and cloud infrastructure changes for security, reliability, cost and deployment risk.
---

When reviewing a cloud infrastructure change:
1. Summarize the proposed change.
2. Identify affected resources and environments.
3. Determine the potential blast radius.
4. Check security and IAM implications.
5. Check availability and disaster-recovery implications.
6. Identify destructive or irreversible operations.
7. Check naming, tagging and organizational standards.
8. Classify findings as Critical, High, Medium or Low.
9. Never deploy or approve the change automatically.
10. Produce a final recommendation:
    - Approve
    - Approve with conditions
    - Reject


A Skill is not model training
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
Creating a Skill does not normally retrain Claude or change the underlying language model.

It provides Claude with:
- procedures;
- rules;
- examples;
- templates;
- domain-specific instructions;
- validation checklists;
- output formats.
Think of it as dynamic procedural knowledge, not permanent neural-network learning.



### What a Skill does not provide:
A Skill does not automatically provide access to:
- GitHub;
- Azure DevOps;
- Jira;
- Google Drive;
- databases;
- Azure or Google Cloud;
- monitoring systems.
That is the Connector’s job.




Skills vs Connectors vs Projects vs Custom Instructions
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
- Four features sound similar and beginners mix them up constantly. 
- Skills and Connectors are the two stars of this course; the other two are their close cousins.


** Skill vs Project. 
- A Project always loads its context the moment you open a chat in it, which is useful but always on. 
- A Skill stays dormant until a request matches it, then activates anywhere, even outside any project.
- Standing background knowledge → Project. 
- A procedure that fires on demand → Skill.

** Skill vs Connector. 
- This is the most important pair and the one you will use most. 
- A Connector is access (what AI can reach); a Skill is expertise (how AI should behave). 
- They are not alternatives; they are partners, which is the whole point of section 8.

** Skill vs Custom instructions. 
- Custom instructions touch everything you do. 
- A Skill touches one kind of task. 
- "Always reply in the same language I write to you in" is a custom instruction. 
- "When I ask for a board paper, use this eight-section structure" is a skill.




Skills: Safe-Use Checklist
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
- Install skills from trusted sources. The built-in Anthropic skills and the official directory are the safe default. A random zip from a forum is not.
- Read a skill before enabling it. 
  Open the SKILL.md and any bundled files. 
  You can even ask AI to do it for you: "Read this skill and tell me exactly what it instructs you to do. 
  Flag anything that contacts external servers, handles credentials, or could leak my data." 
  Pay special attention to scripts and to any instruction that reaches out to the internet.
- Start connectors read-only.
   Grant "search and summarize" before "send" or "create" or "delete." Move up to write access only after you have watched the tool behave well.
- Scope to the smallest folder or app the task needs. 
  Don't connect everything; don't grant whole-drive access for a one-folder job.
- Before allowing AI to edit, move, or delete files, confirm how that specific connector handles recovery, version history, and undo. 
  Some write actions may overwrite or bypass the recovery path you expect, so keep your own backups of anything you can't afford to lose.
- On a team, route shared skills through your organization's directory, where an owner has reviewed them, rather than passing zips around.
































Anatomy of a SKILL.md
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
Every skill is the same simple structure. Here's a minimal version of our accountant skill;

- The file must be named exactly SKILL.md.
- A SKILL.md is the main instruction file inside a Skill folder. It tells Claude:
  * What the Skill is
  * When the Skill should be activated
  * How the task should be performed
  * What tools and supporting files may be used
  * What the final result should look like

- A basic Skill has this structure:
azure-infrastructure-review/
├── SKILL.md
├── references/
│   ├── security-standards.md
│   └── azure-patterns.md
├── examples/
│   └── sample-review.md
└── scripts/
    └── validate-terraform.py

# Only SKILL.md is essential. Reference files, examples, templates, assets and scripts are optional.
# Two main parts

SKILL.md
│
├── 1. YAML Frontmatter
│      Metadata describing the Skill
│
└── 2. Markdown Body
       Instructions Claude follows


YAML frontmatter = When and how the Skill activates
Markdown body = What Claude must do after activation
Supporting files = Detailed knowledge, examples and executable helpers


Its anatomy is:
SKILL.md
├── YAML frontmatter
├── Purpose
├── When this applies | When to use
├── Required inputs
├── Process | Workflow or procedure
├── Confirmed-versus-assumed rules | Decision criteria
├── Output format | Output format
├── Ground rules | Rules and guardrails
├── Delivery requirements
├── Supporting resources
└── Link to example | Examples



meeting-notes-organizer.skill
└── meeting-notes-organizer/
    ├── SKILL.md
    └── examples/
        ├── sample.md
        └── trigger-prompts.md


Basic example: (Markdown)
======================================================================================================================================================
---
name: azure-infrastructure-review
description: Review Azure and Terraform changes for security, reliability, cost and deployment risk.
---

# Azure Infrastructure Review

Review the supplied Azure or Terraform changes.

## Procedure
1. Summarize the proposed changes.
2. Identify affected Azure resources.
3. Review security and RBAC implications.
4. Identify destructive operations.
5. Review reliability and availability.
6. Estimate cost implications.
7. Classify findings by severity.
8. Provide an approval recommendation.



Part 1: YAML frontmatter
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
- The section between the two --- lines is called YAML frontmatter.
- This is the only thing AI keeps loaded at all times. 
- It's how AI decides whether this skill is relevant: progressive disclosure, level one.

---
name: azure-infrastructure-review
description: Review Azure and Terraform changes for security, reliability, cost and deployment risk.
---

- Frontmatter is metadata, not the main task procedure.
- Claude uses it to understand:
  * the Skill’s identity;
  * what it does;
  * when it should be activated;
  * how it can be invoked;
  * which tools it can use;
  * whether it should run in the main conversation or a separate agent context.

Important frontmatter fields:

| Field                      | Purpose                                          | Example                                   |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| `name`                     | Human-readable Skill name                        | `azure-infrastructure-review`             |
| `description`              | Explains what it does and when to use it         | `Review Azure and Terraform changes...`   |
| `when_to_use`              | Gives additional activation examples             | `Use for PR reviews and deployment plans` |
| `argument-hint`            | Shows the expected command input                 | `[pull-request-number]`                   |
| `arguments`                | Defines named input parameters                   | `[issue, environment]`                    |
| `allowed-tools`            | Tools Claude may use when the Skill is active    | `Read Grep Bash`                          |
| `disallowed-tools`         | Tools that must not be used                      | `Write Edit`                              |
| `disable-model-invocation` | Prevents automatic activation                    | `true`                                    |
| `user-invocable`           | Determines whether it appears as a slash command | `false`                                   |
| `context`                  | Runs the Skill in a separate context             | `fork`                                    |
| 'agent'                    | Selects a subagent when using forked context     | `Explore`                                 |
| `model`                    | Selects a model for the current Skill execution  | Model identifier                          |
| `effort`                   | Selects the reasoning-effort level               | `high`                                    |
| `paths`                    | Restricts automatic activation to matching files | `**/*.tf`                                 |


name
======================================================================================================================================================
name: azure-infrastructure-review

- This gives the Skill a readable identity.
- For Claude Code Skills stored here:  .claude/skills/azure-infrastructure-review/SKILL.md
- the directory name normally determines the slash command: /azure-infrastructure-review
- The frontmatter name is mainly the display name.


description
======================================================================================================================================================
description: Review Azure and Terraform changes for security, reliability, cost and deployment risk.

- This is the most important metadata field.
- Claude initially sees the Skill description and uses it to decide whether to load the complete instructions.
- The description field is the whole game
- If you remember one thing about building skills, remember this: the description decides whether your skill ever fires. 
- AI doesn't read your instructions to decide relevance; it reads only the description. 
- A vague description means a skill that never triggers; a sharp one means it activates exactly when it should.
- The description in SKILL.md acts like the Skill’s activation rule. Claude uses it to decide:
  * whether to load the Skill;
  * whether the request belongs to another Skill;
  * whether to answer normally without using any Skill.

- A handy debugging trick once a skill is installed: ask AI, "When would you use my client-summary skill?" 
- It will paraphrase the description back to you. 
- If its answer is narrower or wider than you intended, you've found exactly what to fix in the description.

- You can also add negative triggers when a skill fires too eagerly: "Do NOT use for one-off calculations or quick questions — only for full month-end reports."

A weak description:
description: Helps with cloud.

A better description:
description: Review Azure, Terraform and Bicep changes for security, RBAC, networking, reliability, cost and deployment risk. 
Use for pull requests, architecture changes and infrastructure deployment reviews.

The second version explains both:
what the Skill does;
when it should be used.

This supports progressive disclosure: Claude can scan short Skill descriptions first and load the complete body only when relevant.


Automatic versus manual invocation
=====================================================================================================================================================
- By default, Claude Code may activate a Skill automatically, or the user may invoke it manually:  /azure-infrastructure-review
- For sensitive operations such as deployment, deletion, committing code or sending messages, use: disable-model-invocation: true

Example:
---
name: production-deployment
description: Deploy an approved release to the production environment.
disable-model-invocation: true
---

- Claude cannot decide by itself to activate this Skill; the user must invoke it. Anthropic specifically recommends this control for workflows with side effects.



Tool permissions
=====================================================================================================================================================
You can specify which tools the Skill may use.
allowed-tools:
  - Read
  - Grep
  - Bash

You can also prohibit tools:
disallowed-tools:
  - Write
  - Edit

For example, a read-only code-review Skill could inspect files but not modify them:

---
name: security-review
description: Review source code for security vulnerabilities without changing files.
allowed-tools:
  - Read
  - Grep
disallowed-tools:
  - Write
  - Edit
---




Part 2: Markdown body
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
- Everything below the closing --- is the Markdown body.
- The Markdown body contains the actual knowledge and working procedure Claude follows.
- The body contains the actual instructions, loaded only when the description matches your request: level two.
- A well-designed body commonly includes these sections.
1. Purpose
2. When to use
3. Required inputs
4. Workflow or procedure
5. Rules and guardrails
6. Output format
7. Decision criteria
8. Examples
9. Supporting resources


Complete example
======================================================================================================================================================
---
name: azure-infrastructure-review
description: Review Azure, Terraform and Bicep changes for security, RBAC, networking, reliability, cost and deployment risk. Use for
  infrastructure pull requests, architecture changes and deployment plans.
argument-hint: "[file-path-or-pr-number]"
allowed-tools:
  - Read
  - Grep
  - Bash
disallowed-tools:
  - Write
  - Edit
---

# Azure Infrastructure Review

## Purpose
Review proposed Azure infrastructure changes before deployment.

## Inputs
Use the supplied:
- Terraform or Bicep files
- Pull-request diff
- Target environment
- Change request
- Architecture documentation

Do not invent missing configuration.

## Procedure
1. Summarize the proposed change.
2. Identify affected subscriptions, resource groups and resources.
3. Review RBAC and managed identities.
4. Review network access and public exposure.
5. Review encryption and secret handling.
6. Identify destructive changes.
7. Review availability and disaster recovery.
8. Review logging, monitoring and alerting.
9. Identify cost implications.
10. Classify each finding by severity.

## Severity
- Critical: Immediate security or production failure risk
- High: Significant risk requiring correction before deployment
- Medium: Important improvement with manageable risk
- Low: Minor improvement or best-practice recommendation

## Guardrails
- Do not modify files.
- Do not deploy infrastructure.
- Do not display credentials or secrets.
- Clearly label assumptions.
- Require human approval for production changes.

## Output Format
### Executive Summary

Provide a concise summary of the change and overall risk.

### Findings
| Finding | Severity | Resource | Risk | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### Final Decision
Select one:
- Approve
- Approve with conditions
- Reject

Explain the reason for the decision.

## Additional Resources
- Read [references/security-standards.md](references/security-standards.md)
  for company security requirements.
- Read [examples/sample-review.md](examples/sample-review.md)
  when an example output is needed.


Example
======================================================================================================================================================
---
name: client-monthly-summary
description: Prepares a monthly client financial summary for an
  accounting firm. Use when the user asks for a "client summary",
  "monthly close", or "month-end report". Formats amounts in the
  firm's reporting currency, groups by expense head, and flags
  tax-withholding items.
---

# Client Monthly Summary

## Settings (edit these to make it yours)

- Reporting currency: your currency (e.g., USD, EUR, NGN)
- Withholding threshold: the amount above which to flag a payment (e.g., 50,000)

## Instructions
When asked to prepare a client summary or monthly close:
1. Format every amount in the reporting currency above, with thousands
   separators (e.g., "1,250,000").
2. Group all line items by expense head. Sort heads by total,
   largest first.
3. Flag any single payment above the withholding threshold above
   with a "⚑ WITHHOLDING" note.
4. Produce the report in exactly four sections, in this order:
   Overview, Income, Expenses by Head, Flags & Notes.

## Example
User: "Prepare the March summary for DEMO Trading."
Result: A four-section report, currency-formatted, withholding lines flagged.

Exercise 1: Trigger a built-in skill (automatic Skill invocation or automatic Skill triggering)
======================================================================================================================================================
======================================================================================================================================================
Task:
- you have to trigger a built-in skill from Claude.
- Claude has already a lot of skills available for use.


Pre-Requisites:
- file-creation option should be enabled in settings
- Confirm file-creation is on in claude (Settings → Capabilities)


Prompt:
"Turn this into a one-slide PowerPoint with a title and three bullet points: [paste any three facts about your work]."


Expected Result:
# You didn't name a skill; just watch the PowerPoint skill fire on its own.



How to perform the exercise:
======================================================================================================================================================
1. Open Claude
   - Go to Claude.ai and start a new conversation.

2. Confirm/Enable file creation
   - Settings → Capabilities → Code execution and file creation
   - Make sure Claude is allowed to create files or artifacts.
   - Toggle button should be enabled for "Code execution and file creation"

# The exact option name may vary slightly depending on your Claude interface.

3. Use the following prompt
Turn this into a one-slide PowerPoint with a title and three bullet points:

- I work with Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud.
- I manage cloud infrastructure, security and deployment workflows.
- I am studying AI-Driven Development to build AI-assisted engineering workflows.


4. Observe what Claude does
Claude should:
- Understand that you need a .pptx presentation.
- Recognize that presentation creation requires its PowerPoint Skill.
- Activate the relevant built-in Skill automatically.
- Generate a one-slide PowerPoint.
- Provide the created file.

# You did not explicitly write


5. Result/Output
What this exercise proves: It demonstrates the following process:

Your prompt
   ↓
Claude identifies the requested task
   ↓
Claude matches the task with a Skill description
   ↓
The PowerPoint Skill is loaded
   ↓
Claude follows its presentation-creation procedure
   ↓
A PowerPoint file is generated

# This is called automatic Skill invocation or automatic Skill triggering.

# Meeting Notes Organizer

## Purpose

Turn messy, informal meeting input into a clean, scannable, and actionable record — without ever inventing information that wasn't actually in the notes. The core tension this skill manages is between two goals that pull in opposite directions: making the output look complete and professional, while staying strictly honest about what was and wasn't actually said. When in doubt, always favor honesty over completeness — a gap clearly labeled "Not specified" is far more useful to the user than a plausible-looking guess.

## When this applies

Use this skill when the user supplies meeting notes, call notes, discussion notes, interview notes, or an unstructured transcript and asks to organize, summarize, structure, or extract actions/decisions from them. This includes requests like "clean this up," "turn this into minutes," or "pull out the owners and deadlines" — the user doesn't need to say the exact phrase "meeting notes."

This skill does **not** apply to: drafting a meeting invite, building an agenda for an upcoming meeting, general meeting-facilitation advice, transcribing audio into text, or summarizing non-meeting content (articles, reports, papers). If the input isn't notes/transcript *from* a meeting that already happened, this skill isn't the right tool.

## Process

Work through the notes in this order:

1. **Read the whole input first** before writing anything, so later mentions of a topic (e.g., a decision that gets revised mid-meeting) correctly override earlier ones.
2. Identify the likely purpose of the meeting from context.
3. Draft a concise summary (3–5 sentences — resist the urge to pad this out).
4. Pull out confirmed decisions — something the group actually agreed on, not something merely floated.
5. Pull out every action item mentioned, however casually phrased ("I'll take a look at X" counts).
6. For each action item, identify the owner *only* if the notes clearly assign it to a named person.
7. For each action item, identify a due date *only* if one is actually stated (a relative date like "by Friday" is fine to keep as-is, or convert to an explicit date if the meeting date is known — don't guess a date that was never mentioned).
8. Note dependencies, blockers, and risks mentioned anywhere in the notes.
9. Note unresolved questions — things the group explicitly flagged as needing a follow-up or decision.
10. Note what important information seems to be missing (e.g., no date was given for the meeting itself, or a decision was discussed but never actually confirmed).

### The line between confirmed and assumed

This is the most important judgment call in the skill, and it's worth being deliberate about it:

- A **decision** belongs in "Decisions Made" only if the notes show the group actually landed on it — not if someone merely proposed or suggested it. "We should probably switch to weekly syncs" is a suggestion; "Team agreed to move to weekly syncs starting next week" is a decision.
- Never assign an owner to an action item unless a person is clearly named in connection with that task. Don't infer ownership from someone merely being the one who raised the topic.
- Never invent a due date. If the notes say "soon" or give no timing at all, the due date is "Not specified" — don't convert vague language into a specific date.
- If you're not sure whether something is a decision or a suggestion, treat it as a suggestion and, if it seems important, surface it in Key Discussion Points or Open Questions instead of Decisions Made.

## Output format

Always produce these sections, in this order:

### Meeting Summary
3–5 sentences covering what the meeting was about and its general outcome.

### Key Discussion Points
The main topics covered, as a bulleted list. Combine points that are really the same topic discussed more than once.

### Decisions Made
Only clearly confirmed decisions. If none were confirmed, say so explicitly rather than omitting the section.

### Action Items
A single table:

| # | Action Item | Owner | Due Date | Priority | Status |
| - | ----------- | ----- | -------- | -------- | ------ |

- Owner / Due Date: use **Not specified** when not given in the notes.
- Priority: one of `High`, `Medium`, `Low`, `Not specified`. Only mark High/Medium/Low if the notes give a real signal (urgency language, explicit priority, or a near-term hard deadline) — otherwise use Not specified rather than guessing.
- Status: one of `Not started`, `In progress`, `Blocked`, `Completed`, `Not specified`. Default to `Not specified` unless the notes indicate otherwise.

### Risks and Blockers
Anything flagged as a risk, dependency, or blocker. If none were mentioned, say so.

### Open Questions
Anything left unresolved that needs a future decision or follow-up.

### Missing Information
Call out anything important that's absent from the notes — e.g. no clear meeting date, an action item with no owner, a decision that was discussed but never confirmed. This section is what lets the user quickly see what to go chase down.

## Ground rules

- Preserve the original meaning — don't editorialize or add opinions about what the team *should* have decided.
- Every meaningful action item should make it into the table, even small ones — but don't split one task into multiple rows just to pad the list.
- Keep language plain and concise; this is a working document, not a narrative.
- Never pull in outside information about the topic, company, or people that wasn't in the supplied notes, even if you happen to know it.
- If the notes are extremely sparse (e.g., just a list of names and one sentence), it's fine for several sections to be short or to say "None mentioned" — don't manufacture content to fill every section out.

## Delivery

After presenting the organized notes in the chat reply, also save the result as a Markdown (`.md`) file and share it with the user via the file-presenting tool, so they have something to download and forward. Use a filename based on the meeting topic and/or date if one is mentioned (e.g. `2026-07-13-product-sync-notes.md`), otherwise `meeting-notes-organized.md`. If the user explicitly asks for a Word document instead, use the docx skill to produce a `.docx` file rather than Markdown.

i used the below prompt:
======================================================================================================================================================
Use the Skill Creator Skill to help me build a custom Skill called **AWS Infrastructure Change Review**.

The Skill should be used whenever I ask Claude to review an AWS, Terraform, CloudFormation or AWS CDK infrastructure change, deployment plan or pull request.

Here is exactly how I want the review performed every time.

## PURPOSE

Review proposed AWS infrastructure changes before deployment and identify security, reliability, operational, cost and deployment risks.

## REQUIRED INPUTS

Use the information available from:

* Terraform, CloudFormation or AWS CDK code
* Pull-request changes or diff
* Target AWS account and environment
* Target AWS Region
* Related change request or ticket
* Architecture documentation
* Company security and compliance standards

If important information is unavailable, clearly identify what is missing. Do not invent configuration details.

## REVIEW PROCESS

1. Summarize the proposed infrastructure change.
2. Identify the affected AWS accounts, Regions, VPCs and resources.
3. Review IAM roles, policies, users and permissions for excessive access.
4. Check for wildcard permissions and violations of least privilege.
5. Review VPC configuration, security groups, network ACLs and public exposure.
6. Review S3 bucket access, public access settings and encryption.
7. Review encryption using AWS KMS.
8. Check secrets management through AWS Secrets Manager or Systems Manager Parameter Store.
9. Identify destructive, replacement or irreversible operations.
10. Estimate the deployment blast radius.
11. Review availability, Multi-AZ configuration, redundancy and fault tolerance.
12. Review backup, recovery and disaster-recovery configurations.
13. Review CloudWatch logs, metrics, alarms and audit logging.
14. Check AWS CloudTrail and AWS Config coverage where relevant.
15. Identify likely cost implications.
16. Review naming, tagging and organizational standards.
17. Classify every finding by severity.
18. Provide a final deployment recommendation.

## AWS-SPECIFIC CHECKS

Review the following where relevant:

* IAM least-privilege configuration
* Root account and privileged access risks
* VPC and subnet architecture
* Public and private subnet placement
* Security group rules
* Open inbound ports
* Public IP addresses
* Internet Gateway and NAT Gateway usage
* S3 public access
* S3 encryption and versioning
* RDS encryption, backup and Multi-AZ configuration
* EC2 instance exposure and IAM instance profiles
* Lambda permissions and environment variables
* API Gateway authorization
* ECS and EKS security configuration
* KMS key policies and rotation
* Secrets Manager usage
* CloudTrail logging
* CloudWatch monitoring and alarms
* AWS Config compliance
* AWS Backup coverage
* Cross-account access
* Resource tagging
* Cost-impacting resources
* Terraform or CloudFormation resource replacement

## SEVERITY LEVELS

* **Critical:** Immediate security exposure, data-loss risk or major production failure risk.
* **High:** Significant risk that should be corrected before deployment.
* **Medium:** Important issue that should be planned for correction.
* **Low:** Minor issue or best-practice improvement.

## OUTPUT FORMAT

Produce the following sections:

1. Executive Summary
2. Proposed Changes
3. Affected AWS Resources
4. Security Findings
5. Reliability and Availability Findings
6. Cost Findings
7. Deployment and Blast-Radius Assessment
8. Required Actions
9. Final Recommendation

Use this findings table:

| Finding | Severity | AWS Resource | Risk | Recommended Action |
| ------- | -------- | ------------ | ---- | ------------------ |

The final recommendation must be one of:

* Approve
* Approve with conditions
* Reject

## MUST-DOS

* Explain why each finding matters.
* Clearly identify assumptions.
* Separate confirmed findings from possible risks.
* Prioritize Critical and High findings.
* Mention missing evidence or configuration.
* Identify AWS account, Region and environment where possible.
* Highlight destructive or replacement operations.
* Check for excessive IAM permissions.
* Check for publicly accessible resources.
* Keep the executive summary concise.

## MUST-NOTS

* Do not deploy infrastructure.
* Do not modify source files unless I explicitly request it.
* Do not execute Terraform, CloudFormation or AWS CDK deployments.
* Do not approve unresolved Critical findings.
* Do not expose AWS credentials, access keys, secrets or tokens.
* Do not recommend using the AWS root account.
* Do not assume that a development configuration is suitable for production.
* Do not claim that a configuration is secure without supporting evidence.
* Do not make changes to AWS accounts or resources.
* Do not ignore destructive Terraform or CloudFormation operations.

## PERMISSIONS

The first version of this Skill must be read-only.

It may:

* Read infrastructure code
* Analyze pull-request changes
* Review Terraform plans
* Review CloudFormation change sets
* Identify risks
* Recommend corrections

It must not:

* Modify files
* Apply Terraform plans
* Deploy CloudFormation stacks
* Run AWS CDK deployments
* Create, update or delete AWS resources
* Change IAM policies
* Modify production systems

## SKILL ACTIVATION

The Skill should activate for requests such as:

* Review this AWS Terraform plan.
* Check this CloudFormation template before deployment.
* Review this AWS CDK pull request.
* Identify security risks in this AWS infrastructure change.
* Assess the blast radius of this AWS deployment.

The Skill should not activate for general learning questions such as:

* What is Amazon EC2?
* Explain AWS Lambda.
* Compare AWS with Azure.
* Help me prepare for the AWS Solutions Architect exam.

Ask me any questions needed to remove ambiguity. After I answer, create the complete Skill package, including:

* `SKILL.md`
* AWS review checklist
* Severity definitions
* Sample expected output
* Three prompts that should trigger the Skill
* Three prompts that should not trigger the Skill

Before finalizing it, review the Skill for unclear instructions, overlapping rules, unsafe permissions and overly broad activation criteria.
